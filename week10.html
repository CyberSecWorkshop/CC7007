CC7007 · Digital Forensics · Anti-Forensics

IP Theft Investigation
Autopsy + Anti-Forensics

Students investigate a suspected intellectual property theft using Autopsy. The case is intentionally incomplete: the suspected steganographic payload may not be recoverable during the workshop. The goal is to identify anti-forensic behaviour, build a defensible hypothesis, and report uncertainty properly.

Tool: Autopsy / The Sleuth Kit
Theme: anti-forensics
Artefacts: disk image, PST emails, docs, images
Duration: 2–3 hours
🧠 WHAT ARE WE DOING?

This workshop is not a simple “find the flag” exercise. It is a forensic reasoning exercise. Students are given a disk image from a shared university drive and email artefacts recovered from a mail server. They must use Autopsy to locate relevant artefacts, reconstruct the communication path, identify anti-forensic techniques, and decide whether the evidence supports suspected intellectual property misuse.

The important teaching point is the boundary between evidence, inference, and unsupported conclusion. The case contains strong indicators of attempted IP theft: suspicious communications, ciphered messages, BitCrypt-related activity, image format conversion, duplicate image versions with different hashes, and possible slack-space artefacts. But the hidden payload is not definitively recovered. That means the correct conclusion is not “IP theft proven”; it is “attempted or likely misuse supported by evidence, with unresolved payload recovery.”

⚠️ Instructor framing

You are not expected to fully decrypt the image or prove the payload. You are expected to document what Autopsy can show, identify anti-forensic techniques, and write a balanced finding. This avoids panic and stops students from inventing certainty.

What students will practise

Autopsy navigation
Add a disk image, run ingest, inspect volumes, search files, examine metadata, extract artefacts, and use timeline/web/email views.
Anti-forensic recognition
Spot deletion, carving, ciphered communication, steganography indicators, misleading passwords, and metadata mismatch.
Evidence triage
Separate relevant artefacts from background noise such as unrelated Enron case files and normal academic material.
Defensible reporting
Write findings with confidence levels, cite artefact locations, and recommend severity without overstating proof.
📁 CASE BRIEFING
// Incident type: suspected insider misuse · Context: university IP · Main tool: Autopsy

A UK university without specialist digital forensics capability asks London Metropolitan University to investigate suspected intellectual property theft. Relevant files from a staff member's shared drive and emails from the mail server have been recovered and placed on a flash memory drive, then imaged by IT staff.

The suspected drive is shared, so attribution is not automatic. Students must avoid assuming that every file belongs to one user. They must reconstruct what happened from artefacts: email content, attachments, documents, image metadata, browsing history, deleted/carved files, and suspicious software.

Your task: investigate the image in Autopsy and produce a management-facing forensic report explaining whether misuse is supported, what anti-forensic techniques appear, and how severe the incident should be treated.

🧩 WHAT IS KNOWN / UNKNOWN
Known from the artefacts
The image contains PST email files, a John/Freddy email thread, ciphered messages, a suspicious Granada BMP attachment, documents including an IP proposal, browser sessions showing BitCrypt research, and BitCrypt-related files.
Not conclusively known
The suspected hidden payload is not recovered. The password “Bushmills” appears to fail. The true embedded content, if any, is not confirmed during the standard workshop.
💡 Correct conclusion model

Good: “The evidence supports likely attempted IP exfiltration using anti-forensic techniques, but the hidden payload was not recovered.”

Bad: “IP theft definitely happened.”

PART 1 — AUTOPSY SETUP AND EVIDENCE BASELINE

⏱ ~20 min · Create case, add disk image, run ingest, verify baseline

Students begin by creating a clean Autopsy case. The first goal is not to “find suspicious stuff”. It is to establish the evidence baseline: what image was examined, what file system was present, whether hashing was verified, and what volumes/data sources Autopsy detected.

What we are finding out
  • What data source was loaded into Autopsy and whether the hash was recorded.
  • What file system and volume structure are present.
  • Which ingest modules were run and what artefact categories are available.
  • Whether the evidence acquisition has limitations because files were placed on a flash drive rather than acquired directly as a bit-for-bit source copy.
🖥 Autopsy
Create New Case → Add Data Source → Disk Image or VM File
Suggested case metadata

Do not alter original evidence. Work only from the provided image.
Recommended ingest modules
  • File Type Identification — required for spotting images, documents, archives, PST files, and renamed files.
  • Embedded File Extractor — useful for archives and compound documents.
  • Recent Activity — useful for browser sessions and web artefacts.
  • Email Parser — useful where PST parsing is available in your Autopsy setup.
  • Keyword Search — use targeted words such as BitCrypt, Granada, Bushmills, IP rights, royalties, Haggerty, Freddy.
  • PhotoRec / file carving options if available — relevant because the case includes carved images and deleted/unallocated artefacts.

PART 2 — EMAIL COMMUNICATIONS AND COVERT MESSAGING

⏱ ~35 min · PST files, email thread, attachment flow, ciphered messages

The email thread is the narrative spine of the case. Students should identify the two PST files, locate the John/Freddy conversation, record dates/times, and identify when the communication changes from ordinary text to unreadable/ciphered text.

✉️
Why email matters here
Intent and concealment are more important than file presence alone

A confidential document sitting on a shared drive is not enough to prove theft. The email thread provides context: discussion of IP rights, money, instructions to leave no traces, a password exchange, and an image attachment sent during the suspicious communication window. That turns isolated artefacts into a coherent investigative hypothesis.

🖥 Autopsy
Views → Data Artifacts → Email Messages / File Views → PST files
What to locate
  • PST containers: locate GetRid.pst and GetRid.pst2 or equivalent parsed email containers.
  • John/Freddy thread: record sender, recipient, subject if available, and timestamp sequence from 12–18 February 2013.
  • Attachment: identify the email where Granada_2012(v3).bmp or a similar Granada BMP image is attached.
  • Cipher transition: identify where readable conversation changes into ciphered/unreadable text.

Task 2.2 — Treat the cipher as anti-forensics

⚠️ Do not present deciphered text as magically proven

If students use an online substitution solver, they must document that this is an external analysis step and not something Autopsy itself proves. The ciphered email is direct evidence of concealment; the decoded meaning is an analytical result that needs validation.

🧩 Analysis
Monoalphabetic substitution hypothesis

Ask students to try simple frequency-analysis thinking before giving them the solved text. They should understand that a substitution cipher hides content but leaves structural traces: repeated letters, word lengths, punctuation and conversational flow.

Possible decoded meaning — instructor-controlled reveal
DO NOT GIVE TOO EARLY
Key ideas reportedly recovered:
- interest in obtaining IP rights
- university would have to pay to get it back / pay royalties
- payment discussion
- instruction to use a tool and leave no traces
- password clue: "Bushmills" — later appears unreliable
⚑ Finding 2 — Covert Communications Identified
Artefact: John/Freddy email thread Technique: Monoalphabetic substitution / ciphered communication Indicators: unreadable messages, suspicious money/IP discussion, "leave no traces", password exchange Autopsy source: [PST path / email artefact ID / attachment] Confidence: High for concealment; Medium for decoded content unless independently validated.
This is anti-forensics by communication obfuscation. It does not destroy evidence, but it attempts to reduce readability and delay interpretation. The correct student finding is not just “encrypted email found”; it is “communication content was deliberately concealed during the suspicious transaction window.”

The disk image contains several documents. Students must identify which documents are potentially relevant to IP theft and which are background or noise. This is evidence triage: not every interesting file matters.

PART 4 — IMAGES, METADATA AND SUSPECTED STEGANOGRAPHY

⏱ ~40 min · Granada image variants, hashes, EXIF, carved images, attachment trail

The Granada image is the central anti-forensics artefact. It appears as a normal personal image, but its presence as both JPG and BMP, its attachment in the suspicious email thread, and its relationship to BitCrypt create a strong steganography hypothesis.

🖼️
Why a normal-looking image can be suspicious
Steganography hides data in plain sight

Encryption hides message content. Steganography attempts to hide the existence of the message. In this case, the suspicious behaviour is not that a Granada image exists. It is that the image appears in multiple formats, one version is attached to suspicious communications, BitCrypt is present, BitCrypt works with BMP files, and the variants reportedly have different hashes despite visual similarity.

🖥 Autopsy
File Types → Images / Keyword Search → Granada
What to compare
  • Granada_2012.JPG — original-looking image. Record EXIF, camera model, date taken, file size, hash.
  • Granada_2012(v3).bmp — BMP attachment or BMP variant. Record file size, hash, timestamps, parent path/email link.
  • Second BMP variant — if present, compare size and hash against the first BMP.
  • Carved images — note whether carved files lack timestamps. That is a limitation, not useless evidence.

⚠️ Interpretation rule

Same visual appearance + same/similar size + different hash does not automatically prove steganography. It is an indicator. Its strength increases when combined with BitCrypt presence, BMP requirement, suspicious email thread, and instruction to leave no traces.

⚑ Finding 3 — Suspected Steganographic Carrier Identified
Carrier candidate: Granada_2012(v3).bmp Supporting artefacts: JPG original / BMP variants / different hashes / email attachment / BitCrypt presence Anti-forensic category: steganography + encryption Payload recovered: [yes/no] Confidence: High that image is suspicious; Medium that it contains exfiltrated IP unless payload recovered.
This is the strongest anti-forensic artefact in the case. The correct conclusion is calibrated: the image is a credible suspected carrier, but the hidden content is not proven unless students recover it.

Task 5.2 — Slack and unallocated space

⚠️ Hard boundary for the workshop

Autopsy can reveal unallocated/carved material and sometimes expose slack-related artefacts, but fully recovering a hidden encrypted payload may require specialist carving, stego analysis, password recovery, or reverse engineering. That is beyond the normal workshop. Do not penalise students for not cracking it.

⚑ Finding 4 — Anti-Forensics Techniques Mapped
Identified techniques: 1. Communication obfuscation: ciphered emails 2. Data hiding: suspected steganography in BMP 3. Encryption: BitCrypt and password requirement 4. Artefact reduction: deletion / carved files / missing timestamps 5. Investigator delay: wrong or misleading password clue Overall assessment: anti-forensic behaviour is strongly supported.
The case is valuable because multiple weak indicators reinforce each other. A single unusual BMP is not enough. A suspicious BMP plus ciphered emails plus BitCrypt plus “leave no traces” plus failed password is a coherent anti-forensics pattern.
PART 6 — STUDENT REPORT DELIVERABLES

⏱ ~35 min · Submit a short management-facing forensic report

Students submit a concise report. It should not read like a raw Autopsy dump. It should explain what was examined, what was found, how anti-forensics affected the investigation, and what severity should be assigned.

Required report sections
  • Methodology: tool used, data source loaded, ingest modules, keyword strategy, limitations.
  • Timeline: event → evidence source → timestamp → confidence.
  • Evidence table: email thread, confidential document, Granada image variants, BitCrypt artefacts, browser sessions.
  • Anti-forensics map: technique → artefact → investigative impact.
  • Conclusion: whether misuse is supported, attempted, or unproven.
  • Severity recommendation: low / medium / high with reasoning.

Severity recommendation

⚖️
How to grade severity without overclaiming
Severity is based on risk and conduct, not only recovered payload

A reasonable recommendation is High severity attempted misuse if students support it with evidence: suspicious IP conversation, instruction to leave no traces, covert communication, BitCrypt research/use, and suspected steganographic transfer. But they should clearly state that completed IP theft is not conclusively proven unless hidden content is recovered.

Suggested conclusion wording
USE / ADAPT
The investigation identified strong indicators of attempted intellectual property misuse and deliberate anti-forensic behaviour. Evidence includes ciphered communications discussing IP rights and payment, instructions to leave no traces, BitCrypt-related artefacts, and a suspicious BMP image attachment with characteristics consistent with a possible steganographic carrier. However, the suspected hidden payload was not recovered during this investigation. Therefore, the evidence supports a finding of likely attempted misuse and anti-forensic concealment, but not definitive proof of completed IP theft.
// Submission — Workshop Output

Submit one report containing: methodology, evidence baseline, timeline, evidence table, anti-forensics map, severity recommendation, and limitations.

Minimum standard: every claim must be tied to a file, email, image, browser artefact, or Autopsy view.

Excellent standard: students distinguish what is directly observed, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.

📋 WHAT DID THIS INVESTIGATION DEMONSTRATE?
1 — Anti-forensics is often cumulative
No single artefact proves the whole case. The pattern emerges from several layers: ciphered email, suspicious attachment, encryption tool, browser activity, hash mismatch, and carved/deleted material.
2 — Steganography hides the existence of the message
A normal-looking BMP image may be the carrier. The investigative problem is not just decrypting content; it is first recognising that hidden content may exist.
3 — Autopsy gives strong triage, not every final answer
Autopsy helps locate emails, documents, metadata, images, web artefacts, and carved files. Advanced stego recovery, password cracking, and reverse engineering may require external tools.
4 — Good forensic reporting includes uncertainty
The strongest report does not exaggerate. It says what is proven, what is likely, and what remains unresolved. That is professional forensic reasoning.
